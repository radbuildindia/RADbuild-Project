MRI Room Planning

MRI Room Planning & Infrastructure Requirements

An MRI suite adds problems a CT room never has — a magnet that is always on, a cryogen vent that must reach outside air, and a fringe field that does not respect walls. RADbuild plans and builds the whole environment.

What makes MRI different

Every constraint that applies to a CT room applies here too: structure, power, earthing, cooling, access, flooring. MRI then adds four that have no equivalent in CT, and each of them can invalidate an otherwise good site.

The magnet is always on

A superconducting magnet does not switch off between patients. The fringe field extends in three dimensions — through walls, through floors, and through the ceiling into whatever is above. Planning has to account for what occupies the adjacent spaces vertically as well as horizontally, and for controlled access at the boundary. Where the field would otherwise extend into occupied or sensitive areas, magnetic shielding is considered as part of the design.

Ferrous everything becomes a hazard

The building itself has to be planned around the field. Structural steel, reinforcement, ductwork, cable trays, fire equipment, door hardware, furniture and the trolleys that will be wheeled in later all need consideration. This shapes ordinary construction decisions long before the equipment arrives.

The quench pipe has to go somewhere

If the magnet quenches, cryogen boils off rapidly and must be vented safely to outside air. The route has to be identified early, because it drives structural penetrations and often the position of the magnet room itself. It must be correctly sized and supported, and the discharge point placed clear of air intakes, windows, walkways and public areas. Oxygen monitoring inside the magnet room is part of the same safety design.

This is regularly the constraint that decides where in a building an MRI can go — and it is regularly discovered far too late.

RF shielding is a room inside a room

The magnet room sits inside an RF cage that keeps external radiofrequency noise out. It affects wall build-up, door position, service penetrations and finishes. See our detailed guide to MRI RF shielding.

Buying a pre-owned or refurbished magnet? The RF cage is almost never part of the deal, and neither is the quench route, the chiller platform or the earthing. A used magnet at an attractive price frequently arrives with the entire infrastructure scope still open. Plan it before you buy, not after.

The rest of the environment

Structure and access

A magnet is heavy and awkward, and the delivery route matters more than in almost any other install. The full path — gate, driveway, entrance, corridors, turning radii, lift capacity, door openings — is surveyed against the actual magnet dimensions, and temporary structural openings or craneage planned where the route will not take it. The slab and beams are assessed for both the resting load and the transit load along that route.

Cooling and the chiller platform

MRI systems have continuous cooling requirements and tight environmental tolerances, and the cooling cannot simply stop. That means chiller and cooling water circuits with appropriate redundancy, and an outdoor platform that is structurally designed, positioned for pipe run length, vibration isolated and left accessible for service.

Power and earthing

Load study against real demand, transformer adequacy, DG backup, UPS for the systems that must not lose power, in-house fabricated LT panels, and a dedicated clean earth within the manufacturer's tolerance — tested and certified.

HVAC, flooring and interiors

Temperature and humidity held inside tolerance, room pressurisation and fresh air, quench venting and oxygen monitoring. Flooring to levelling tolerance with cable routing planned into trenches or false flooring, then the control room, reporting area, changing rooms and patient areas finished properly.

MRI suite interior fit-out

Interiors inside an RF cage are constrained in ways ordinary fit-out is not. Every finish, fixing and fitting in the magnet room has to be compatible with both the shield and the magnetic field.

  • Non-ferrous fittings throughout the magnet room — furniture, brackets, door hardware and fixtures selected for the field
  • Finishes applied inside the RF cage without compromising shield continuity
  • Flooring to levelling tolerance, with the floor shield protected during equipment move-in
  • Control room — console space and sightlines through the RF window
  • Patient areas — changing, waiting and screening space positioned outside the controlled zone
  • Ceilings and lighting compatible with the cage and the field

Fixings matter most here too: drilling into a shielded wall after certification breaches the cage. Interior work inside the magnet room is planned into the build, not added to it. See RF shielding.

Where the numbers come from

Magnet weights, room dimensions, fringe field plots, cryogen vent sizing and environmental tolerances are specific to the model and field strength, and they change between document revisions. We work from the current pre-installation documentation for your exact system, obtained through your equipment supplier. We do not republish manufacturer specifications.

Questions

MRI room planning, answered.

What is the biggest thing owners miss on an MRI project?
The quench pipe route. It drives structural penetrations and often dictates where in the building the magnet can go, yet it is frequently considered only after the room location has been fixed.
I am buying a refurbished MRI. What is not included?
Typically the RF cage, quench routing, chiller platform, dedicated earthing and the room itself. The magnet price is rarely the project price, and the gap is usually the infrastructure.
Can an MRI go on an upper floor?
Sometimes — it depends on structural capacity, the delivery route, and whether the quench vent can reach outside air on a viable path. All three need answering before the location is fixed.
What is the fringe field and why does it affect my neighbours?
The magnetic field extends beyond the magnet in three dimensions, including above and below. Planning must consider what occupies those adjacent spaces, with controlled access at the boundary and magnetic shielding where required.
Do you handle RF shielding yourselves?
Yes — design, supply, installation, and leakage testing with certification on completion.
